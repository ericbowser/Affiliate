# Sunstone Knoll Utah: Complete Collecting Guide

*Last updated: August 2026 · By Wasatch Rockhound · 7 min read*

Sunstone Knoll is the easiest site on this list, full stop. No hard-rock cracking, no long dirt roads, no digging pits — just a short walk onto a small volcanic knoll south of Delta where sunstones glitter right on the surface. If you want to test whether rockhounding is actually for you before committing to a bigger trip, this is where to start.

Here's everything you need to plan a trip: what sunstones actually are, exactly how to get there, and one thing about the land itself that's worth understanding before you go.

---

## Quick Facts

- **Location:** Millard County, off Highway 257, south of Delta
- **GPS:** 39.147°N, 112.717°W
- **Distance from SLC:** ~2.5 hours
- **Land Status:** Private mining claim overlapping BLM land — collecting is allowed, sign the register
- **Best Season:** Year-round — aim for a sunny day
- **Difficulty:** Easy
- **Vehicle:** Any vehicle handles the short dirt entrance road

---

## What You'll Find

**Sunstones** — technically a transparent, yellowish variety of labradorite feldspar, though most collectors just call them sunstones. They catch the light with a warm golden shimmer that makes them genuinely easy to spot once your eyes adjust to what you're looking for. Most pieces run from about 1/8 inch up to nearly an inch; larger stones exist but are rare.

The knoll itself is a small volcanic cinder cone that erupted roughly 750,000 to 1.6 million years ago. Sunstone crystals weathered out of that basalt and now sit loose across the flats on the east side of the knoll, or remain embedded in cavities within the rock itself for anyone willing to break a few pieces open.

---

## A Note on the Land

This one's worth understanding before you go: Sunstone Knoll sits on a mix of private mining claim and BLM public land. The claim holder generously allows public rockhounding here — this isn't a legal guarantee the way collecting on straightforward BLM land is, it's a privilege extended to visitors. There's a visitor register at the entrance; sign it. Treat the site with the same respect you'd want if it were your own claim, and this access stays open for the next person.

## How to Get There

**From Salt Lake City:** Head south on I-15 to Nephi, then continue toward Delta via US-6/50.

**From Delta:** From the west edge of town (the railroad overpass), go west on US-6/50 for about 4.3 miles to the junction with State Highway 257. Turn south on 257 and continue roughly 13.4 miles — you'll see a sign on the west side of the road marking the Sunstone Knoll entrance just before mile marker 56, on the east side.

Turn east onto the dirt entrance road and cross the railroad tracks — **check for trains before crossing** — then follow the road as it circles around to the east side of the knoll, where most of the collecting happens. (Some references describe this same spot as "about 11 miles south of Deseret" — same location, different reference point.)

The entrance road and the short loop onto the knoll are dirt but well-traveled and flat; any vehicle handles it in normal conditions.

## Where to Collect

The east side flats are the main event. Walk slowly and look for the glint — sunstones catch sunlight in a way that's genuinely easy to pick out once you know what you're looking for. Morning and evening light tend to show the glitter best.

If the flats feel picked over, or you're visiting on a cloudy day when surface hunting is harder, look for basalt with visible cavities and break it open with a [rock hammer](/review/estwing-rock-hammer) — crystals and fractured grains occur inside the rock itself, not just on the surface.

**One safety note specific to this site:** broken glass litter on the flats gets mistaken for sunstone more often than you'd expect. If you pick up a piece that doesn't quite look right, it probably isn't a sunstone — and packing it out anyway is a nice thing to do for the next visitor.

---

## When to Go

Sunstone Knoll is collectible year-round — there's no seasonal road closure or access issue like the more remote sites on this list. The main variable is light: sunny days make surface hunting dramatically easier, since you're relying on the crystals catching the sun. Morning and evening sun angles tend to produce the best glitter.

There's zero shade on the knoll, so in summer, treat this like any other exposed desert site — go early, bring more water than you think you need.

## Rules and Etiquette

- Sign the visitor register at the entrance
- This is a mix of private claim and BLM land — collect reasonable personal quantities, don't treat it as a commercial resource
- Watch for rattlesnakes in the brush around the flats
- Check for trains before crossing the railroad tracks at the entrance
- Pack out trash — including any broken glass you find mistaken for sunstone

## FAQ

**Is Sunstone Knoll free to collect?**
Yes, in the sense that there's no fee — but it's not simple public BLM land either. Part of the site is a private mining claim where the owner allows public collecting. Sign the register and collect reasonable personal quantities.

**Is this a good first rockhounding trip?**
About as good as it gets. Easy access, no special gear required for surface hunting, visible results, and a short enough drive that it doesn't demand a full day.

**Can kids go?**
Yes — this is one of the most kid-friendly sites in the state. Flat terrain, immediate visible finds, and no hard-rock cracking required unless you choose to do it.

**Do I need a permit?**
No permit is required for the personal-quantity collecting this guide describes. (You may see boilerplate language elsewhere about Utah State Trust Land permits — that's general information about a different land category, not a requirement specific to this site.)

**What's the best time of day to visit?**
Morning or evening, when the lower sun angle makes the sunstones' glitter easiest to spot. Midday sun works too, just with slightly more effort.
