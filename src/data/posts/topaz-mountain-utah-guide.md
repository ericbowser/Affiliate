# Topaz Mountain Utah: Complete Collecting Guide

*Last updated: October 2026 · By Wasatch Rockhound · 10 min read*

Topaz Mountain is the single best free rockhounding site in Utah — and arguably one of the best in the country. Utah made topaz its official state gemstone in part because of this mountain, and once you've spent a morning cracking rhyolite on the south slope you'll understand why.

This is the complete guide to collecting at Topaz Mountain: how to get there, where to dig, what you'll actually find, and the mistakes that waste a trip. I've made this drive from Salt Lake City more times than I can count, and what follows is everything I wish I'd known the first time.

- **Location:** Thomas Range, Juab County, Utah
- **GPS:** 39.666°N, 113.125°W

- **Land Status:** BLM — free, no permit required

- **Difficulty:** Moderate
- **Vehicle:** High-clearance strongly recommended
- **Cell Service:** None — bring a satellite communicator

## What You'll Find

Topaz Mountain sits on the southern end of the Thomas Range — a volcanic formation that erupted roughly six to seven million years ago. The topaz formed inside gas cavities (called vugs) within the rhyolite, and that's where you'll be looking.

**Topaz** — The main attraction. Crystals are typically amber to colorless, prismatic, with a glassy luster that's unmistakable once you've seen it. Most specimens run under an inch, but larger pieces do turn up. Topaz has a Mohs hardness of 8, so it's extremely durable and takes a beautiful polish if you're into lapidary work — though honestly, the raw crystals are usually more impressive left as-is.

**Garnet** — Deep red almandine garnets show up in the rhyolite matrix. They're small (typically 2-5mm) but vivid. Not gem-quality in most cases, but satisfying to find and great in a display collection.

**Opal** — Opal nodules occur in the rhyolite, ranging from common opal (milky, no fire) to occasional pieces with genuine play-of-color. Don't expect Australian-grade fire opal, but the pieces you find are worth keeping.

**Amethyst** — Purple quartz shows up occasionally. Not the primary find here, but a nice bonus when it appears.

**Red Beryl** — Exists at Topaz Mountain but is extraordinarily rare. Red beryl is worth more per carat than diamond and is found in only a handful of locations worldwide. In a dozen trips I've found only a few tiny pieces. Don't plan your trip around this — consider it a lottery ticket.

**Pseudobrookite** — Small, dark, metallic crystals that form alongside the topaz. Mineral collectors prize these. Most visitors walk right past them, which means more for you.

## How to Get There

**From Salt Lake City:**

Take I-15 south to Nephi (Exit 222). Head southwest on UT-132 for about 34 miles to Lynndyl and the junction with US-6. Continue south on US-6 for about 5 miles, then turn west onto Highway 174 (Brush Wellman Road), which is well marked. Follow the paved road northwest for roughly 37 miles to the signed dirt road that leads up to Topaz Mountain. The mountain is clearly visible from that turn, and the most popular collecting is on its southern slope. (These directions follow the BLM's own description of the route.)

The final stretch is unpaved BLM road. In dry conditions, a truck or SUV with decent clearance handles it fine. After rain, this road becomes rutted and potentially impassable — check conditions before you go.

**GPS waypoint:** 39.666°N, 113.125°W will put you at the main collecting area on the south side of the mountain.

The BLM notes there is no water and no services at the mountain. Fill up in Nephi and bring everything you need.

## Where to Dig

The south side of Topaz Mountain is the primary collecting area, and within that there are distinct zones worth knowing.

### The South Slope Washes

The easiest collecting for beginners. Walk the dry washes on the mountain's south face and look for loose topaz crystals that have weathered out of the rhyolite naturally. Bring a spray bottle — topaz is dramatically easier to spot when wet because the glassy surface catches sunlight in a way that dry, dusty crystals don't.

Work slowly. The crystals are often partially buried in the wash gravel. A small hand rake and a [1/8" classifier screen](/review/classifier-screen-18) are your best tools here. Sift the gravel and check for the characteristic glassy prismatic shape.

### The West Wall

Historically the most productive hard-rock area. This is where you'll use a [rock hammer](/review/estwing-rock-hammer) and [cold chisel](/review/cold-chisel-set) to crack open rhyolite and look for crystal-lined vugs. The vugs are gas cavities in the volcanic rock — some are empty, some contain topaz, garnet, or opal.

The tradeoff: the west wall gets the heaviest foot traffic because it has the reputation. On a busy spring weekend, you'll have company. The easy vugs have been cracked open many times over.

### The East Wall

Less picked over than the west wall, and in my experience, increasingly productive as more collectors crowd the western side. The geology is the same — rhyolite with crystal-bearing vugs — but fewer people make the walk over. If you're willing to put in the extra effort, the east wall rewards it.

### The Saddle Area

The saddle between Topaz Mountain and the adjacent peak has produced good specimens, particularly garnets and pseudobrookite. Less topaz here, but the mineral variety is higher.

## Collecting Techniques

### Surface Hunting (Easiest)

Walk the washes and slopes, spraying water ahead of you. Topaz crystals that have weathered free of the rhyolite will be sitting in the gravel and soil. This technique works best in the early morning when the low-angle sun catches the wet crystal surfaces.

**Pro tip:** After a rain (once the road dries enough to drive), surface collecting is dramatically better. Rain washes new crystals out of the soil and cleans the surface layer, making specimens much easier to spot.

### Screening

Bring a small screen ([1/8" classifier](/review/classifier-screen-18)) — for sifting the wash gravel. This catches the smaller crystals you'd walk right past. Some of the prettiest topaz from this site is under 1/4 inch — too small to spot by eye but perfect for a mineral cabinet.

### Hard-Rock Cracking

This is where the best specimens come from, but it takes more effort and equipment. Look for rhyolite with visible vugs or cavities. Use a [pointed rock hammer](/review/estwing-rock-hammer) and [cold chisel](/review/cold-chisel-set) to carefully open the vugs. Work slowly — a careless swing destroys the crystals you're trying to extract. Wear [wrap-around safety glasses](/review/dewalt-safety-glasses) every time you swing.

The key is reading the rock. Look for:
- Small holes or pits in the rhyolite surface (these often indicate vugs below)
- Color changes in the rock (darker areas sometimes surround crystal pockets)
- Rounded bumps on the surface (can indicate underlying geode-like formations)

## When to Go

**Best months:** April–May and September–October.

**Why not summer?** Topaz Mountain is fully exposed desert at roughly 6,500 feet elevation. June through August temperatures regularly hit 95-100°F with zero shade. Heat exhaustion is a real risk, and the harsh midday sun makes surface hunting nearly impossible — the glare washes out crystal surfaces.

**Why not winter?** Snow and ice make the access road dangerous to impassable from roughly November through March. The mountain itself can have significant snow cover.

**Best time of day:** Early morning (sunrise to about 10 AM) and late afternoon (4 PM to sunset). The low-angle light is better for spotting crystals, and the temperatures are manageable even in shoulder-season heat.

**After rain:** The absolute best surface collecting happens 2-3 days after a rain event — long enough for the access road to dry out, fresh enough that newly exposed crystals haven't been picked up by other collectors. Watch the weather forecast and plan accordingly.

## Camping at Topaz Mountain

Camping overnight is one of the best ways to get the most out of a Topaz Mountain trip. A sunrise start on the mountain beats a 5 AM departure from Salt Lake City every time.

**Dispersed camping is permitted throughout the Topaz Mountain Rockhound Recreation Area.** There are no developed campsites, no reservations, and no fees. The BLM allows free dispersed camping on all BLM Utah-managed lands except where posted, and Topaz Mountain has no posted restrictions.

### What to Know

- **14-day limit** — You can camp up to 14 days in one spot before you must move at least 25 miles. Not a concern for a weekend trip, but worth knowing if you're spending a week.
- **No water, no facilities** — There is nothing at the mountain. The BLM notes one pit toilet on the access road, but that's it. Bring all your water, food, and fuel.
- **Camp in previously disturbed sites** — BLM rules require you to use existing disturbed areas. Don't clear new ground, trench, dig, or level your site.
- **Fire rules** — Use existing fire rings or a fire pan. Collect only dead and down wood. Never leave a fire unattended. Check [utahfireinfo.gov](https://utahfireinfo.gov/fire-restrictions/) for current fire restrictions before you go — summer and early fall often bring fire bans in western Utah.
- **Pack out everything** — There are no trash services. Leave No Trace applies fully.

### Camping Gear to Bring

- A [headlamp](/review/black-diamond-spot) — essential for camp after dark and early-morning starts
- A [multi-tool](/review/leatherman-signal) — the fire starter and emergency whistle earn their weight out here
- Warm layers — desert temperatures drop sharply at night, even in shoulder season. Expect 30s–40s°F overnight in April, May, September, and October.
- A ground tarp and sleeping pad — the ground is rocky volcanic soil
- Extra water beyond your collecting supply — plan at least 1 gallon per person for camp use on top of what you'll drink during the day

### Nearest Developed Camping

If dispersed camping isn't your style, the closest developed options are about 50 miles away:
- **Yuba State Park** — ~50 miles east, with developed sites and reservations
- **Antelope Valley RV Park** (Delta) — full-hookup RV sites, a good staging area if you're towing

## Rules and Etiquette

**BLM collecting rules:**
- 25 pounds per person per day for personal (non-commercial) use
- **Topaz collected in the recreation area cannot be sold** — collection is for personal, casual use only
- **Hand tools only** — no power tools or heavy equipment. The BLM banned power tools due to degradation of site characteristics
- Do not enter tunnels or caves — exercise caution on steep terrain
- Pack out all trash — leave the site cleaner than you found it

**Etiquette:**
- Don't dig within someone else's active area
- Backfill your holes — other collectors and wildlife will thank you
- If you crack open a vug and find crystals, that's your find — but don't claim an entire cliff face
- Share information freely — the rockhounding community is generous and tight-knit

**[BLM Topaz Mountain page](https://www.blm.gov/visit/topaz-mountain)** and **[Topaz Mountain Rockhound Recreation Area](https://www.blm.gov/visit/topaz-mountain-rockhound-recreation-area)** — check both for access updates or closures before you go. The BLM Fillmore Field Office can answer road-condition questions at (435) 743-3100.

## FAQ

**Is Topaz Mountain free to collect?**
Yes. It's BLM land — no permit, no fee, no registration. The 25 pound per person daily limit for personal collecting applies. No commercial collecting without a BLM permit.

**Can I drive a sedan to Topaz Mountain?**
I wouldn't recommend it. The final stretch is unpaved BLM road with rocks, ruts, and washouts. A truck, SUV, or crossover with decent ground clearance handles it fine in dry conditions. After rain, even high-clearance vehicles can struggle on the clay sections.

**Is there cell service at Topaz Mountain?**
No. You'll lose signal well before you reach the mountain. Bring a [satellite communicator](/review/garmin-inreach-mini-2) if you're going solo — the nearest help is hours away.

**What's the best time of year to go?**
April-May and September-October. Avoid summer heat (95-100 degrees with no shade) and winter snow (access road can be impassable November-March).

**Can I camp at Topaz Mountain?**
Yes — free dispersed camping is permitted throughout the recreation area with a 14-day limit. There are no developed campsites, no water, and no facilities beyond a pit toilet on the access road. Pack everything in, pack everything out. See the [Camping at Topaz Mountain](#camping-at-topaz-mountain) section above for full details and gear recommendations.

**How much topaz will I actually find?**
On a typical day of surface hunting and screening, expect to find 10-30+ small crystals, with most under 1/2 inch. Hard-rock cracking can produce larger specimens but is hit-or-miss. Manage expectations: you won't fill a bucket, but you'll come home with genuine Utah topaz.

**Can kids go?**
Absolutely. Surface hunting and screening in the washes is kid-friendly (ages 8+). Avoid the hard-rock cracking areas with children — flying rock chips are a real hazard. Bring extra water and plan for a shorter day.
