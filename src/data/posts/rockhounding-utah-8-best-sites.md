# Rockhounding in Utah: 8 Best Sites + What You'll Find

*Wasatch Rockhound · Updated May 2026*

---

Utah is one of the best rockhounding states in the country — and it's not particularly close. Where most states have a handful of productive public-land sites, Utah has hundreds. The geology here is absurd in the best possible way: ancient seabeds folded into desert mountains, volcanic fields that erupted millions of years ago and left behind topaz and obsidian, and river systems that have been quietly sorting agates and jaspers for eons.

I live in Salt Lake City and I've been working my way through the state's BLM sites for the better part of three years, mostly in the western desert. What follows are the eight sites I'd send a friend to first — ranked loosely by how beginner-friendly they are, with honest notes on access, what you're likely to actually find, and what to bring.

One note before we go: **always verify land status before you go.** Most of these sites are on BLM land where casual collecting is free and legal. I've linked the relevant BLM pages where they exist. The rules are simple — 25 pounds per day for personal use, no commercial collecting, no heavy equipment.

## 1. Topaz Mountain (Thomas Range, Juab County)

**What you'll find:** Topaz, garnet, amethyst, opal, red beryl (rare), pseudobrookite
**Distance from SLC:** ~3.5 hours
**Best season:** April–May, September–October
**Difficulty:** Moderate — high-clearance vehicle strongly recommended for the last mile

If you're going to do one Utah rockhounding trip, this is it. Topaz Mountain sits on the southern end of the Thomas Range in Juab County and is one of the premier free topaz-collecting sites in the world. Full stop.

The topaz here formed inside gas cavities in ancient rhyolite — a volcanic rock that erupted roughly six to seven million years ago. Utah actually designated topaz as its state gemstone, and standing on this mountain it's easy to see why. The crystals are typically amber to colorless, small (most run under an inch), and prismatic with that characteristic glassy luster.

**How to find them:** Work the washes and slopes on the south side of the mountain. Bring a spray bottle — topaz is dramatically easier to spot when wet because the glassy surface catches the light. For better specimens, bring a rock hammer and chisel and crack open the rhyolite looking for crystal-lined vugs (cavities). The west wall is historically the most productive but gets heavy traffic; the east wall is less picked over.

Beyond topaz, keep your eyes open for deep red garnets, opal nodules in the rhyolite, and the occasional piece of purple amethyst. Red beryl exists here too — it's worth more per carat than diamond and is almost impossibly rare — but don't count on it. In a dozen trips I've found only a few tiny pieces.

**What to bring:** A [rock hammer and chisel](/category/rock-hammers), a spray bottle, a fine-mesh screen (1/8") for sifting the topaz-rich soil, sunscreen, and at minimum two gallons of water per person. There are no services of any kind out here.

**Land status:** BLM — free, no permit required. [BLM Topaz Mountain page](https://www.blm.gov/visit/topaz-mountain)

## 2. Dugway Geode Beds (Tooele County)

**What you'll find:** Geodes lined with quartz (clear, amethyst, rose), calcite
**Distance from SLC:** ~2.5 hours
**Best season:** March–May, September–November
**Difficulty:** Moderate — long dirt road, bring a spare tire

The Dugway Geode Beds might be the most satisfying beginner rockhounding experience in Utah because the reward is guaranteed: crack open a gray, unremarkable-looking ball of rock and find sparkling crystal inside. My first trip here I came home with a bucket of geodes I spent the evening opening on the back porch with a cold chisel. It's immediately addictive.

The geodes are typically 2–4 inches in diameter, dark gray, spherical, and hiding in the soft unconsolidated tuff of the eroded hillsides. The BLM manages two main excavation pits, but collecting isn't limited to the pits — walk a quarter mile off the main parking area and you'll usually find less-picked ground.

**How to find them:** Dig into the soft hillside material — it's a gray, loose matrix that a shovel handles easily. Look for round shapes; they often pop right out. Surface specimens exist but tend to be more weathered. For the best crystals, dig into the embedded layer on the hillside faces rather than just surface hunting.

**Opening your geodes:** You can crack them with a hammer and burlap sack (messy but fast), score them with a chisel for a cleaner break, or take them home to a rock saw. Most local rock shops will cut them for a small fee if you want the clean flat display face.

**Getting there:** From SLC, take I-80 west to exit 99 (Tooele/SR-36), then south on SR-36 for ~40 miles to the Pony Express Road just past Faust. Turn west and drive 50 miles to the geode bed turnoff. That last stretch is a dirt road — passable in a regular truck in dry conditions, but after rain the clay becomes impassable. GPS: 39.9300°N, 113.1000°W.

**What to bring:** A shovel (non-negotiable), a [rock hammer and chisel](/category/rock-hammers), safety glasses, a spare tire, and plenty of water. The nearest gas is in Dugway.

**Land status:** BLM — free, no permit required. [BLM Dugway Geode Beds page](https://www.blm.gov/visit/dugway-geode-beds)

## 3. U-Dig Fossils (Millard County, near Delta)

**What you'll find:** Trilobite fossils — *Elrathia kingii*, *Asaphiscus wheeleri*, and others
**Distance from SLC:** ~2.5 hours
**Best season:** April–October (check their hours before going)
**Difficulty:** Easy — this is a managed fee site with tools provided
**Fee:** ~$70/person for a full day

This one is a little different — it's a private quarry rather than a BLM free site — but it earns its spot on this list because of how productive it is. U-Dig sits in the Wheeler Shale about 52 miles west of Delta, and the Wheeler Shale is one of the most prolific trilobite deposits in North America.

Trilobites are extinct marine arthropods that lived 500+ million years ago, when Utah sat beneath a shallow inland sea. *Elrathia kingii* is the most commonly found species here — up to 2 inches long, beautifully preserved, and something that stops people cold when they realize how old it actually is. You get to keep everything you find.

The quarry staff are knowledgeable and will show you the splitting technique. You're working through thin shale layers with a chisel and brush — it's meditative and occasionally spectacular. The owner's deep knowledge of the site makes this worth the fee even if you could find trilobites on nearby free BLM land.

**Pro tip:** Combine this with Topaz Mountain or the Dugway Geode Beds into a two-day Millard County loop — the area is one of the richest rockhounding regions in the state.

**What to bring:** Tools are provided, but comfortable knee pads, sunscreen, and more water than you think you need.

## 4. Tintic Mountains (Juab County)

**What you'll find:** Garnets, silver minerals, lead minerals, fluorite
**Distance from SLC:** ~1.5 hours
**Best season:** April–November
**Difficulty:** Moderate — mix of BLM and old mining district land; verify access before collecting

The Tintic Mountains are the closest serious rockhounding destination to Salt Lake City, sitting about 1.5 hours south near the town of Eureka. This is old mining country — the Tintic Mining District was one of Utah's most productive silver and lead districts in the 19th century — and the tailings and exposed geology left behind make for productive hunting.

Garnet is the main draw for mineral collectors, but the broader area yields fluorite, galena, cerussite, and the occasional quartz specimen. Because this is a historic mining district, pay attention to claim markers — active claims are off limits, and some of the old mine shafts are genuinely dangerous. Stay out of structures and don't collect from clearly posted private land.

**What to bring:** A [rock hammer](/category/rock-hammers), a hand lens for identifying minerals in the field, and a printed topo map — cell coverage is patchy. The *Rockhounding Utah* field guide by Gretchen Hazlewood covers several specific Tintic sites with directions.

## 5. San Rafael Swell (Emery County)

**What you'll find:** Agate, jasper, chalcedony, petrified wood, Moqui marbles, fossil coral
**Distance from SLC:** ~3 hours (via I-70)
**Best season:** March–May, September–November
**Difficulty:** Moderate to hard — vast area, requires navigation, some roads require 4WD

The San Rafael Swell is a massive geological dome east of I-15 in central Utah — about 75 miles long and 40 miles wide — where tectonic forces folded ancient sedimentary layers into canyons, ridges, and mesas that expose hundreds of millions of years of rock. For rockhounds it's a candy store, but it requires more preparation than the western desert sites.

The San Rafael River gravel bars are one of the most productive areas, yielding agate, jasper, and chalcedony tumbled smooth by water — exactly the kind of material that polishes beautifully in a rock tumbler. Buckhorn Wash turns up petrified wood and occasional ammonite fossils. Moqui marbles (iron-oxide concretions that look like stone balls) appear across the eroded desert flats and are one of the stranger finds you'll make in the field.

**What to bring:** A [Garmin eTrex or similar GPS unit](/review/garmin-etrex-32x) is nearly essential here — the road network is confusing and unmarked, and getting turned around in the Swell with a dead cell battery is not a fun afternoon. Extra fuel, a recovery strap, and a [satellite communicator](/review/garmin-inreach-mini-2) if you're going deep.

**Land status:** Mostly BLM — Bureau of Land Management, Price Field Office manages the area.

## 6. Marysvale (Piute County)

**What you'll find:** Fluorite, amethyst, quartz, cinnabar, alunite, opal
**Distance from SLC:** ~3.5 hours
**Best season:** May–October
**Difficulty:** Easy to moderate depending on which area you're working

Marysvale sits in the Piute County canyon country along the Sevier River and is one of those towns that old-time rockhounds know well but beginners usually miss. The entire area around Marysvale is productive — almost any creek, mine tailings pile, or wash holds something interesting.

Fluorite is a standout find here: cubic crystals in purple, green, and clear that make beautiful display pieces. Cinnabar (mercury sulfide, the bright red mineral) appears in some of the old mine dumps — striking to look at, and worth handling minimally given the mercury content. Amethyst quartz shows up in several locations around the hills.

**Note:** Some of the most productive areas are adjacent to old mining operations. Stay on BLM land, respect private property boundaries, and don't collect from active or clearly posted claim areas.

**What to bring:** A rock hammer, gloves (especially if you're handling cinnabar), and the *Gem Trails of Utah* field guide which has specific Marysvale site directions.

## 7. Sunstone Knoll (Millard County)

**What you'll find:** Sunstones (oligoclase feldspar with aventurescence)
**Distance from SLC:** ~2.5 hours
**Best season:** April–October
**Difficulty:** Easy — flat desert terrain, short walk from parking

Sunstone Knoll is one of only a handful of places in the United States where you can reliably find sunstones — a variety of feldspar that glitters with a warm golden or reddish metallic shimmer from tiny copper platelets inside the crystal. These aren't the Oregon sunstones that gem cutters rave about, but Utah sunstones are genuine collectors' pieces and an unusual find.

The site sits in the desert southwest of Delta, and the sunstones are found on the surface eroding out of a basaltic cinder cone. Just walk the ground slowly and look for the glitter. No digging required — this is pure surface hunting.

Because it's an easy, flat site with immediate visible rewards, Sunstone Knoll is an excellent first rockhounding trip for kids or for someone who wants to test the hobby before committing to a longer desert drive.

**What to bring:** Good eyes, a small container, and comfortable walking shoes. The site is exposed with zero shade — go early or late in the day in summer.

**Land status:** BLM — free, no permit required.

## 8. Comb Ridge / Moses Rock (San Juan County)

**What you'll find:** Pyrope garnets ("Arizona Rubies"), chlorite, chromite
**Distance from SLC:** ~4.5 hours
**Best season:** March–May, September–November
**Difficulty:** Moderate — remote, limited services in the area

If you want garnets — genuine, deep red pyrope garnets that look like rubies when held to the light — Comb Ridge and Moses Rock in San Juan County are where to go. These garnets are sometimes called "Arizona Rubies" in the trade even though they're not rubies at all, just particularly vivid pyrope garnets eroding out of an ancient kimberlite-type rock called a minette.

They're small — most are 3–6mm — but the color is exceptional, and they tumble to a high polish. This is the kind of find that makes non-rockhounds do a double-take when you show them what you picked up off the ground.

The drive down US-163 through Monument Valley en route is spectacular, and there's enough to see in the broader area (Valley of the Gods, Bears Ears) to make this a proper weekend trip rather than a day dash.

**What to bring:** A [fine-mesh screen and hand sieve](/category/field-gear) for sifting the gravel where garnets concentrate, a GPS, extra fuel, and a full overnight kit if you're combining with the Monument Valley area.

## What to Bring on Any Utah Rockhounding Trip

A few non-negotiables regardless of which site you're heading to:

**Tools:** A quality rock hammer is the single most important tool. The [Estwing 22oz rock pick](/review/estwing-rock-hammer) is what most serious rockhounds carry — indestructible, well-balanced, and made in the USA. Bring a cold chisel for splitting rock, and a small brush for clearing debris.

**Water:** Utah's west desert will dehydrate you faster than you expect, especially in spring and fall when temperatures feel mild. Two liters minimum for a day hike. On longer or hotter trips, carry more. A [quality insulated bottle](/category/field-gear) keeps water cold all day.

**Navigation:** Most of these sites require dirt road driving with no cell service. A [dedicated GPS unit](/review/garmin-etrex-32x) preloaded with topo maps is more reliable than a phone. For remote sites (San Rafael Swell, Comb Ridge), a [satellite communicator](/review/garmin-inreach-mini-2) is worth the investment — it's the difference between a misadventure and a rescue.

**Vehicle:** High-clearance is a strong recommendation for most of these sites. You don't necessarily need 4WD in dry conditions, but low clearance will leave you stuck or damaged on several of these approach roads.

**Field guides:** The two worth keeping in the truck are *[Rockhounding Utah by Gretchen Hazlewood](/review/rockhounding-utah-book)* (75 sites with GPS coordinates, the definitive Utah reference) and *[Gem Trails of Utah by James Mitchell](/review/gem-trails-utah)* (older but strong on the southern and central sites). Between the two you'll have directions to more sites than you can get to in a season.

## Final Thoughts

Utah rewards preparation. Most of these sites are genuinely remote — no gas, no water, no cell service, no one coming to help if you get a flat or get stuck. That's also exactly what makes them worth the drive. You'll have Topaz Mountain's south slope to yourself on a Tuesday in April, and the quiet out there — just wind, desert, and the occasional hawk — is as good as what you find on the ground.

Start close and build out. The Tintic Mountains at 1.5 hours from Salt Lake are a perfect first trip. Dugway at 2.5 hours is where the hobby becomes an obsession. By the time you make the drive to Comb Ridge, you'll already know you're not stopping.

Get out there.
