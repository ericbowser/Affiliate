# Dugway Geode Beds Utah: Complete Collecting Guide

*Last updated: August 2026 · By Wasatch Rockhound · 9 min read*

The Dugway Geode Beds might be the most satisfying beginner rockhounding site in the entire state, and the reason is simple: the reward is guaranteed. Crack open a gray, unremarkable ball of rock and find sparkling quartz crystal inside. There's no skill required to find the geodes themselves — just a shovel and a willingness to dig.

This is the complete guide to collecting at Dugway: how to get there, where to dig, how to open what you find, and everything else I wish I'd known before my first trip out on the Pony Express Road.

- **GPS:** 39.930°N, 113.100°W
- **Distance from SLC:** ~2.5 hours
- **Land Status:** BLM — free, no permit required

- **Difficulty:** Moderate
- **Vehicle:** High-clearance recommended — bring a spare tire
- **Cell Service:** None — bring a satellite communicator

## What You'll Find

The geodes at Dugway formed inside gas cavities in ancient volcanic ash (tuff) that's since eroded into soft, unconsolidated hillsides. They're typically 2–4 inches in diameter, dark gray, and roughly spherical — completely unremarkable on the outside, which is exactly why so many people walk right past productive ground.

**Quartz geodes** — The main find. Clear to milky quartz crystals lining the cavity interior, sometimes in dramatic druzy clusters.

**Amethyst geodes** — Less common but present. Purple quartz crystal linings that make for a genuinely striking display piece once cut and polished.

**Rose quartz geodes** — Occasional pink-tinted crystal interiors. A nice surprise when you crack one open.

**Calcite** — Shows up alongside the quartz in some specimens, often as a secondary crystal layer.

You won't know what's inside until you open it, which is a big part of what makes this site so addictive. My first trip I came home with a bucket of geodes and spent the whole evening opening them on the back porch with a cold chisel.

## How to Get There

**From Salt Lake City:**

Take I-80 west to Exit 99 (Tooele/SR-36). Head south on SR-36 for roughly 40 miles to the Pony Express Road just past the town of Faust. Turn west onto Pony Express Road and drive approximately 50 miles to the geode bed turnoff.

That last 50-mile stretch is dirt road. It's passable in a regular truck under dry conditions, but the clay surface becomes genuinely impassable after rain — check the forecast before you commit to the drive.

**GPS waypoint:** 39.930°N, 113.100°W will put you at the main collecting area.

**No gas, no water, no services** exist along Pony Express Road once you leave Faust. Fill up before you turn off SR-36.

## Where to Dig

BLM manages two main excavation pits at the site, and most first-time visitors never leave them — which is exactly why it's worth knowing what's beyond.

### The Main Pits

The easiest collecting for beginners. The soft tuff walls are already exposed, and geodes weather out of the material on a regular basis. Dig into the hillside face rather than just scanning the ground — surface specimens tend to be more weathered and less crystal-rich than what's still embedded in the layer.

### Beyond the Pits

Walk a quarter mile or more off the main parking area and you'll typically find less-picked ground. The geode-bearing layer isn't limited to the two managed pits — it runs through the surrounding hillsides. If the main pits feel picked over on a busy weekend, this is where to go.

## Collecting Techniques

### Digging

This is a shovel site, not a hammer-and-chisel site — at least for finding the geodes themselves. The gray, loose tuff matrix gives way easily to a [folding camp shovel](/review/collapsible-field-shovel). Look for round shapes as you work through the material; geodes often pop free of the surrounding tuff with surprisingly little effort once you've exposed one.

**Pro tip:** Work the hillside faces, not just the pit floor. The embedded layer produces better, less-weathered specimens than material that's already tumbled down and been picked over.

### Opening Your Geodes

You've got three options once you're home:

1. **Hammer and burlap sack** — Wrap the geode in burlap (or an old towel) and strike it with a rock hammer. Fast and effective, but messy and unpredictable — you don't control where the break falls.
2. **Score with a chisel** — Use a [cold chisel](/review/cold-chisel-set) to score a line around the geode's circumference, tapping gradually to work a cleaner break. More control, more patience required.
3. **Rock saw** — Most local rock and lapidary shops will cut geodes for a small per-piece fee if you want the clean, flat display face rather than a jagged hammer break.

Wear [safety glasses](/review/dewalt-safety-glasses) any time you're striking rock — chips fly, and geodes are no exception.

## When to Go

**Best months:** March–May and September–November.

**Why not summer?** The site sits fully exposed in open desert with zero shade. Summer heat plus a shovel and physical digging is a bad combination — this is more physically demanding than surface hunting at a site like Topaz Mountain.

**Why not winter?** The 50-mile dirt approach on Pony Express Road becomes unreliable with any snow or moisture, and the clay sections turn to an impassable mess.

**After rain:** Give the road several days to dry out completely before attempting the drive — the clay here holds moisture longer than it looks like it should.

## Rules and Etiquette

**BLM collecting rules:**
- 25 pounds per person per day for personal (non-commercial) use
- No power tools or heavy equipment
- No commercial collecting without a permit
- Pack out all trash — leave the site cleaner than you found it

**Etiquette:**
- Backfill the holes you dig — loose pits are a hazard for the next visitor and local wildlife
- Don't dig within someone else's active pit
- Share information freely — the rockhounding community here is generous

**[BLM Dugway Geode Beds page](https://www.blm.gov/visit/dugway-geode-beds)** — check for any access updates before you go.

## FAQ

**Is Dugway free to collect?**
Yes. It's BLM land — no permit, no fee, no registration. The 25 pound per person daily limit for personal collecting applies.

**Can I drive a sedan to Dugway?**
Not recommended. The 50-mile dirt approach on Pony Express Road is manageable for a truck or SUV in dry conditions, but the clay sections after rain can strand even high-clearance vehicles.

**Is there cell service at Dugway?**
No, for most of the drive and at the site itself. Bring a [satellite communicator](/review/garmin-inreach-mini-2) if you're going solo.

**Do I need any tools to find geodes, or just dig?**
Just a shovel to find them. You'll want a hammer, chisel, or access to a rock saw later to open what you find.

**How many geodes will I actually find?**
A few hours of steady digging in productive ground typically produces a bucket's worth. Not every geode will have a spectacular interior — some are mostly solid or calcite-filled — but finding *something* is close to guaranteed here, which is what makes it such a good beginner site.

**Can kids go?**
Yes — this is one of the more kid-friendly sites on this list. Digging is straightforward and low-risk compared to hard-rock hammering, and the payoff of cracking open a geode at home is a great activity in itself. Bring extra water and expect a shorter day than an adult group would plan.
